# The Formed Life Translation

## Style Guide — Version 2.0

**Project:** Formed Life Translation  
**Document type:** Operational style, wording, presentation, and editorial rules  
**Governing authority:** Subordinate to the FLT Constitution v2.0  
**Supersedes:** Style Guide v1.0

> **Dynamic expression is the normal posture. Formal departure needs no special defense; the meaning carried into the English always does.**

This Style Guide converts the Constitution’s governing principles into repeatable translation practice.

When this guide conflicts with the Constitution, the Constitution governs. When this guide is silent, the translator or agent must apply the nearest relevant constitutional principle and escalate unresolved questions to the human editor rather than inventing a local rule.

---

# 1. Why Version 2.0 Exists

Version 1 tended to produce English closer to the CSB or NIV than to FLT’s intended NLT-side position.

The problem was not an explicit command to translate formally. The problem was that formal wording became the safest default under uncertainty. Dynamic moves were treated as exceptions that needed justification, while the dangers of dynamic translation were emphasized more strongly than the dangers of excessive literalness.

Version 2.0 reverses that imbalance.

The governing shift is:

> **Reach the meaning in the clearest natural English. Do not preserve Greek form merely because it is available.**

A rendering may fail in either direction:

- **too loose:** it adds, removes, narrows, expands, embellishes, or resolves more than the Greek warrants;
- **too literal:** it leaves Greek structure, abstraction, religious vocabulary, or compressed logic undigested in the English.

Both are translation failures.

---

# 2. FLT Voice and Style Aim

FLT should sound:

- clear;
- natural;
- direct;
- warm without becoming sentimental;
- emotionally honest;
- mature;
- orally readable;
- contemporary without becoming trendy;
- simple without sounding childish;
- recognizably dynamic.

FLT should not sound:

- archaic;
- academic;
- stiff;
- dependent on church vocabulary;
- mechanically literal;
- slangy;
- cute;
- juvenile;
- over-explained;
- ornamentally paraphrased.

A reader familiar with both the NLT and the CSB should ordinarily recognize FLT as nearer the NLT in naturalness and degree of restructuring.

This comparison is for later editorial calibration only. Existing English translations must not be supplied to blind drafting agents.

---

# 3. Dynamic Expression Is the Default

Dynamic restructuring is the ordinary working mode of FLT.

The translator may freely:

- reorder words and clauses;
- divide or combine sentences;
- change nouns into verbs;
- change grammatical category where natural English requires it;
- convert passive constructions into active ones;
- clarify implied subjects, objects, and referents;
- translate idioms by their intended meaning;
- unpack compact Greek constructions;
- make supported logical relationships explicit;
- replace abstract constructions with natural verbal language;
- vary the English rendering of a Greek term when context requires it;
- recreate emphasis, contrast, repetition, and rhetorical force through natural English forms.

These moves do not need justification merely because they change form.

Their resulting meaning must remain defensible from the Greek and its context.

## 3.1 Dynamic Expression Breaks the Tie

For purposes of this guide, two renderings are **equally faithful** when both pass the Warrant Test. Literal resemblance to Greek does not earn extra faithfulness credit after that threshold is met.

When two renderings both pass the Warrant Test:

- choose the dynamic rendering when it is clearer;
- choose the dynamic rendering when it is more natural;
- choose the rendering that better carries tone, emphasis, and rhetorical force;
- either may stand when they are genuinely equal in meaning, clarity, naturalness, tone, and force.

Do not manufacture difference merely to sound distinctive.

A formal rendering being understandable is not enough reason to keep it when a more natural rendering communicates equally well or better.

## 3.2 Dynamic Does Not Mean Loose

Dynamic expression never authorizes:

- unsupported meaning;
- doctrinal expansion;
- invented motive;
- ornamental color;
- emotional exaggeration;
- commentary;
- unnecessary certainty;
- removal of a meaningful image or argument link.

FLT is free in form and disciplined in meaning.

# 4. Reading-Level Target

## 4.1 Operating Aim

The reading view should ordinarily fall near a sixth-grade reading level.

Flesch-Kincaid, Lexile, and similar measures are diagnostic tools. They are not governing authorities.

A grade level of 7 or higher triggers review for:

- Greek-shaped syntax;
- long sentence structure;
- clause stacking;
- abstract noun clusters;
- traditional religious vocabulary;
- unnecessarily rare words;
- hidden formal equivalence.

It does not automatically fail the passage.

## 4.2 What May Not Be Done to Lower a Score

Do not lower a readability score by:

- chopping coherent sentences into fragments;
- deleting necessary connectors;
- replacing precise words with vague ones;
- selecting childish vocabulary;
- flattening rhythm;
- weakening meaning;
- damaging paragraph flow;
- removing repetition that carries rhetorical force.

When the numerical score conflicts with natural, clear, dignified English, qualitative judgment governs.

## 4.3 Difficulty in the Idea Versus Difficulty in the Prose

A difficult theological idea may remain difficult.

A sentence should not remain difficult merely because the Greek sentence was difficult.

Before accepting elevated readability, confirm that:

- the difficulty comes from the thought itself;
- no abstract noun can become a natural verb;
- no long sentence should be divided;
- no referent remains unnecessarily unclear;
- no religious label could be translated by meaning;
- paragraph cohesion would not be damaged by simplification.

---

# 5. Sentence Structure and Clause Flow

Prefer short-to-moderate sentences, but do not impose a rigid word-count rule.

## 5.1 Review Triggers

The following are review flags, never automatic failure rules:

- a sentence longer than about 25 words;
- more than two nested dependent levels;
- three or more abstract nouns in one clause;
- a chain of participial or subordinate constructions;
- a subject separated from its main verb by more than about 12 words;
- a subject or main verb delayed so long that a listener may lose the thread;
- several pronouns with competing possible referents;
- heavy alliteration or repeated sound patterns that may cause stumbling aloud.

A sentence that remains below a numerical threshold may still fail. A sentence that exceeds one may still succeed.

A longer sentence may remain when:

- its movement is clear;
- its rhythm is natural;
- its logic is easy to follow;
- division would weaken emphasis or cohesion;
- the source itself builds toward a sustained rhetorical effect.

## 5.2 Sentence Division

Long Greek periods should normally be divided when English comprehension improves.

When dividing:

- preserve the logical sequence;
- preserve the author’s emphasis;
- maintain the relationship between propositions;
- add connectors only when English requires them;
- avoid turning one argument into unrelated statements.

## 5.3 Sentence Combination

Very short Greek units may be combined when separate sentences would sound choppy or obscure their connection.

Combining must not:

- erase contrast;
- hide a shift in speaker or subject;
- merge independent claims that should remain distinct;
- weaken emphasis.

---

# 6. Logical Connectors

Use connectors such as *because, so, therefore, but, yet, however, then, still,* and *meanwhile* only when the relationship is sufficiently established.

A connector may be supplied when:

1. the relationship is signaled by Greek grammar, particles, discourse structure, or strong contextual evidence; and
2. natural English requires the relationship to be stated for clear forward movement.

Do not supply a connector when two or more materially different relationships remain plausible. Preserve the openness or escalate the decision.

Do not turn:

- a possible relationship into a certain one;
- sequence into cause;
- result into purpose;
- explanation into conclusion;
- contrast into contradiction.

Do not delete connective tissue merely to lower a readability score.

Do not overuse connectors when the English relationship is already obvious.

---

# 7. Paragraph and Discourse Cohesion

Paragraphs follow discourse movement, not verse numbers.

A paragraph break may signal:

- a new topic;
- a new speaker;
- a new scene;
- a new stage in an argument;
- movement from explanation to exhortation;
- a rhetorical turn;
- an emotional shift.

The paragraph is the primary unit of clarity.

A passage fails even when every sentence is individually clear if the reader cannot tell how the sentences relate.

Chapter-level review should ask:

- Does each paragraph have a recognizable movement?
- Does each sentence follow naturally from the previous one?
- Are changes of subject, time, speaker, or argument stage clear?
- Has sentence splitting weakened the larger argument?
- Is repetition functioning as cohesion or merely sounding redundant?
- Does the paragraph build toward the same emphasis as the Greek?

---

# 8. Clarification Versus Commentary

Clarification belongs in the reading text when it communicates meaning already carried explicitly or implicitly by the Greek and is needed for natural English comprehension.

Commentary begins when the rendering adds:

- historical background not carried by the sentence;
- doctrinal synthesis;
- explanatory theology;
- application;
- motive not stated or adequately implied;
- harmonization with another passage;
- interpretation beyond what the context warrants.

## 8.1 Permitted Clarification

Examples include:

- naming an implied subject when English would otherwise be unclear;
- identifying a pronoun referent;
- making a strongly supported cause or purpose relation explicit;
- translating an idiom by meaning;
- unpacking a compact theological phrase into natural English;
- replacing an abstract noun with a verbal expression;
- supplying an object required by English.

## 8.2 Expansion Review Threshold

There is no absolute word-count ceiling for clarification because Greek meaning may require different amounts of English expression.

However, when unpacking one Greek word or compact construction requires more than one English phrase or dependent clause, the expansion must be flagged for independent warrant review. A separate sentence of explanation normally belongs in a footnote or study layer unless the sentence is itself the natural English translation of a larger Greek construction.

The reviewing agent must identify:

- the exact Greek support for every added semantic element;
- which words are required by natural English;
- whether any part is background, synthesis, or application rather than translation.

The drafting agent may propose the expansion but may not certify its own expansion as non-commentary.

The boundary remains semantic, but extended expansions receive a mandatory second review.

---

# 9. The Two-Direction Dynamic Check

Every proposed rendering must be reviewed for both excessive freedom and excessive literalness.

## 9.1 Too-Loose Check

A rendering is too loose when it:

- adds unsupported meaning;
- removes or weakens source meaning;
- narrows or expands the claim;
- resolves meaningful ambiguity without warrant;
- becomes more expressive than the Greek;
- adds drama, humor, color, or emotional force;
- substitutes explanation for translation;
- loses a textual or theological anchor.

## 9.2 Too-Literal Check

A rendering is too literal when it:

- preserves Greek word order that sounds unnatural in English;
- carries participle chains into English unchanged;
- uses abstract nouns where ordinary English would use verbs;
- keeps compressed logic that English readers cannot follow;
- retains church vocabulary as an opaque label;
- preserves a passive that obscures a clear actor without rhetorical reason;
- keeps a formal rendering merely because it is adequate;
- places the meaning in a footnote while leaving the formal label in the body;
- sounds like a report about the Greek rather than natural communication to the reader.

A too-literal review may not be passed by assertion alone. When a formal rendering is retained in a passage that offered real restructuring freedom, the Translation Packet must preserve at least one plausible dynamic alternative and the reason it was rejected.

The drafting agent may flag possible stiffness but may not certify its own wording as free from stiffness. That judgment belongs to an independent reviewer or the human editor.

## 9.3 Symmetry Rule

Reviewers must look in both directions with equal seriousness.

A workflow that checks only for looseness will steadily drift toward formal equivalence.

A workflow that checks only for literalness will drift toward paraphrase.

---

# 10. Preserving Form That Carries Meaning

Greek form is preserved or functionally recreated when it carries communicative force.

This includes:

- deliberate repetition;
- metaphor;
- imagery;
- contrast;
- parallelism;
- irony;
- emotional intensity;
- rhetorical questions;
- argument structure;
- shifts in tone;
- memorable phrasing;
- meaningful ambiguity;
- connections across a paragraph or book.

The translator asks:

> **What is this form accomplishing?**

Preserve the same form when it works naturally in English.

Recreate the effect in another English form when direct imitation would fail.

Incidental Greek form remains free to change. The presence of repetition, metaphor, or parallel syntax does not automatically require one-to-one formal preservation.

## 10.1 Repetition

Preserve or functionally recreate repetition when it:

- structures an argument;
- creates emphasis;
- links a passage;
- develops a theme;
- creates rhetorical force.

Do not mechanically assign one English gloss to every repeated occurrence. Preserve the repeated function, not necessarily identical wording, when contextual senses differ.

Vary wording when repetition is merely incidental Greek usage or when identical English would mislead.

## 10.2 Metaphor

The default is to preserve a living metaphor unless the Contextual Lexical Matrix, established precedent, or human editor directs otherwise.

Preserve a metaphor when:

- the author develops it;
- it carries theological meaning;
- it links to another part of the passage;
- it remains understandable or can be made understandable through context or a brief note.

Translate an image by meaning only when:

- it functions as a dead idiom rather than an active image;
- preserving it would seriously mislead the target reader;
- the intended meaning is well established;
- no developed literary or theological connection would be lost.

A drafting agent must not erase a major biblical image merely because it judges the image unfamiliar.

---

# 11. Theological and Religion-Naive Vocabulary

The default is to render the meaning.

Retention is the choice that must earn its place.

A traditional theological term may remain when:

- the target reader can reasonably understand it;
- the term is worth teaching because it recurs and carries an important concept;
- replacing it would destroy a theological or literary anchor;
- the Contextual Lexical Matrix directs its retention.

When retained, it must be made intelligible through:

- context;
- a brief first-use note;
- a weighted-term footnote;
- the study layer.

Terms not assumed to be understood include:

- justification;
- sanctification;
- propitiation;
- redemption;
- righteousness in a technical sense;
- flesh in a Pauline sense;
- grace as specialized church language;
- gospel as church jargon;
- saints;
- elect;
- glory as a technical concept.

These examples do not predetermine their final rendering in every context. The Contextual Lexical Matrix governs contextual choices.

## 11.1 Render-and-Teach

FLT should generally:

- render the meaning in the body;
- teach the traditional term in a note when helpful.

It should not generally:

- retain the traditional term in the body;
- force the reader to learn the term before understanding the sentence;
- place the actual meaning only in a note.

## 11.2 Contextual Consistency

Consistency means accountable use across contexts, not mechanical repetition.

A Greek term may receive different English renderings when:

- its contextual sense differs;
- its discourse function differs;
- a repeated English word would mislead;
- natural English requires variation.

A repeated term should remain visibly connected when the author is deliberately developing it.

---

# 12. Significant Meaning

A meaning is significant when a competent reviewer would notice a material difference in what the passage claims, who participates, how the ideas relate, what the passage emphasizes or feels like, or what theological or literary conclusion it supports.

Significance includes changes affecting:

- the main proposition or a necessary implication;
- actor, receiver, speaker, audience, or referent;
- cause, purpose, result, condition, contrast, inference, or sequence;
- scope, degree, time, agency, or certainty;
- theological content or doctrinal implication;
- tone, emphasis, emotional force, metaphor, or rhetorical effect;
- meaningful ambiguity;
- sentence, paragraph, or argument cohesion.

A difference is not significant merely because the English form changed.

Minor recasting that does not materially affect these features does not require separate warrant documentation.

When uncertain whether a difference is significant, flag it rather than dismiss it.

---

# 13. Person, Number, Gender, and Pronouns

## 13.1 Pronoun Clarity and Ambiguity

Use a name or noun instead of a pronoun when a listener may not know the referent on first hearing.

Do not repeat a name unnaturally when the referent is already clear.

Review any pronoun:

- separated from its antecedent by a sentence boundary;
- following a change of subject;
- competing with another possible antecedent;
- whose referent matters theologically or narratively.

When pronoun clarity conflicts with meaningful ambiguity, semantic discipline governs. Do not resolve the ambiguity merely by inserting a name. Restructure without resolving it if possible; otherwise escalate.

## 13.2 Singular and Plural “You”

English does not visibly distinguish singular and plural *you*.

When plurality matters, use natural expressions such as:

- all of you;
- each of you;
- both of you;
- among you;
- your whole group;
- you together.

Do not mark every plural occurrence mechanically.

Preserve singular address when it matters.

Person and number should remain available in study or alignment data.

## 13.3 Gender Rendering

Render masculine generics by intended meaning.

Examples:

- ἀδελφοί addressing a mixed congregation may become “brothers and sisters”;
- generic “man” may become “person,” “people,” or a plural construction;
- generic singular “he” may become plural “they” when natural.

Do not neutralize masculine language referring to:

- God;
- Christ;
- a specific male person;
- a male role or relationship when the gender is part of the meaning.

Do not insert inclusive language where the Greek refers specifically to men.

## 13.4 Person Reformulation

Changing grammatical person may alter scope or rhetorical force and therefore requires caution.

A general third-person statement should not normally become a direct second-person statement merely for vividness. Such a change may be considered only when:

- the source already functions as direct address in context;
- English naturally requires the reformulation;
- scope, force, and audience remain unchanged;
- an independent reviewer confirms that the result does not become more accusatory, moralizing, or individualized.

Otherwise preserve the source person or use a neutral plural construction.

---

# 14. Active and Passive Voice

Active English is preferred when:

- the actor is known with sufficient confidence;
- naming the actor is supported;
- active voice improves clarity;
- emphasis can be preserved;
- the passive has no meaningful rhetorical function.

Retain or recreate the passive only when at least one of the following materially matters:

- the actor is unknown;
- the actor is deliberately backgrounded;
- the receiver of the action is the discourse focus;
- naming God would resolve an intended ambiguity;
- the passive carries theological or rhetorical force;
- the source deliberately avoids identifying the actor.

The mere fact that a Greek verb is passive does not prove that English passive is preferable.

A possible divine passive is not automatic permission to insert “God.”

Supplying an actor must be supported by context and must not erase meaningful ambiguity.

# 15. Oral Comprehension

Every finalized chapter must receive human oral review.

AI agents may flag oral-flow risks, but they must not certify oral quality merely by claiming that a sentence “sounds natural.”

## 15.1 AI Pre-Screening Flags

AI reviewers should flag:

- sentences longer than about 25 words;
- subjects separated from their main verbs by more than about 12 words;
- three or more abstract nouns in one clause;
- unclear sentence boundaries;
- pronouns whose referents become unclear when heard;
- repeated sound clusters or heavy alliteration;
- garden-path sentences;
- overloaded subordinate structure;
- awkward transitions;
- accidental repetition;
- likely misplaced emphasis;
- wording that resembles translated or institutional prose rather than ordinary adult speech.

These are review signals, not mechanical verdicts.

## 15.2 Human Oral Review

Human review should listen for:

- breath length;
- forward comprehension;
- cadence;
- emphasis;
- referent clarity;
- emotional landing;
- stiffness;
- places where the listener loses the subject or main point.

Text-to-speech may assist the process, but human listening remains controlling.

## 15.3 The Stiffness Test

A sentence may be grammatically clear and still fail if it sounds like it is reporting the Greek rather than speaking naturally to the listener.

When a sentence sounds translated:

1. return to the Greek meaning;
2. identify what the source is communicating;
3. rebuild the English sentence naturally;
4. confirm that no meaning was lost or added.

The drafting agent may suggest a stiffness revision but may not approve its own revision as having passed the stiffness test.

## 15.4 Unit-Level Oral-English Smoothing Gate

Oral review must not wait until the full chapter is assembled. After the human editor creates a provisional synthesis, the unit enters a bounded Oral-English Smoothing Pass before exact-candidate audit.

The controlling question is:

> What prevents a listener from understanding this naturally on one hearing, and what is the smallest faithful change that removes that obstacle?

This is comprehension repair, not permission to beautify, modernize, intensify, or paraphrase without a concrete problem.

## 15.5 Listener-Only Diagnosis

The first smoothing stage receives only the selected English. It may flag:

- stacked or nested clauses;
- sentences that exceed a natural breath;
- clusters of prepositions;
- delayed subjects, verbs, or main assertions;
- repetitive sentence openings that fatigue the ear;
- abstract-noun chains;
- parenthetical explanation embedded in the reading text;
- unclear referents;
- weak sentence landings;
- accidental repetition;
- places where the listener loses the paragraph's main point.

It must distinguish deliberate rhetorical repetition from accidental English repetition. It diagnoses friction but does not rewrite the text or guess what the Greek says.

## 15.6 Source-Aware Repair Record

The source-aware stage may propose only targeted repairs. Every proposed change must record:

- current exact wording;
- identified oral problem;
- proposed exact wording;
- form change used;
- possible meaning risk;
- source warrant;
- effect on logic and rhetoric.

Permitted form changes include sentence division or combination, reordering, moving the main assertion forward, replacing an abstract noun phrase with a natural verb, varying an English transition, and moving secondary explanation into a footnote.

A complete proposed verse list must accompany the change record. Any difference not named in the record is a silent change and must be rejected by the harness.

## 15.7 Constitutional Smoothing Checks

Before a smoothed candidate may be sealed, reviewers must check:

1. semantic propositions;
2. cause, contrast, purpose, result, and sequence;
3. deliberate repetition, escalation, irony, and emphasis;
4. meaningful ambiguity;
5. agency, voice, force, and certainty;
6. key-term and verbal-link continuity;
7. traceability to Greek semantic units;
8. whether an English-only back-translation recovers substantially the same meaning;
9. copyright independence;
10. whether the revision is genuinely clearer on a second oral test.

The human editor alone approves exact wording. The approved candidate then receives the independent semantic-floor audit.

# 16. Punctuation and Mechanics

## 16.1 General Punctuation

Use contemporary American English punctuation.

- Use the Oxford comma.
- Prefer periods to semicolons.
- Use semicolons rarely.
- Use em dashes sparingly and without surrounding spaces.
- Avoid heavy parentheses in the reading view.
- Use exclamation points only when the source tone supports strong emotional force.
- Do not use punctuation merely to imitate Greek punctuation.

## 16.2 Em Dashes

Use an em dash for:

- genuine interruption;
- sudden rhetorical turn;
- compact explanatory insertion;
- controlled dramatic emphasis.

Do not use em dashes as a substitute for restructuring an overloaded sentence.

## 16.3 Quotation Marks

Use standard American quotation marks for speech.

Nested quotations use single quotation marks.

Old Testament quotations are governed by §21.

---

# 17. Contractions

Contractions are permitted when natural.

Examples include:

- don’t;
- doesn’t;
- can’t;
- I’m;
- you’re;
- it’s.

Avoid contractions when they:

- weaken solemnity;
- sound flippant;
- reduce rhetorical weight;
- create an inappropriate casual tone;
- interfere with memorable phrasing.

Contractions should be reconsidered during chapter-level read-aloud review.

---

# 18. Capitalization

## 18.1 Pronouns Referring to Deity

Capitalize personal pronouns referring clearly to God, Christ, or the Holy Spirit:

- He;
- Him;
- His;
- Himself;
- You;
- Your;
- Yours.

Do not capitalize relative pronouns:

- who;
- whom;
- whose.

This is an FLT style convention, not a grammatical or theological requirement.

Do not use capitalization to silently settle a genuinely disputed referent. Important ambiguity should be recorded and footnoted when reader-facing significance warrants it.

## 18.2 Spirit and spirit

Use **Spirit** for the Holy Spirit.

Use **spirit** for:

- the human spirit;
- disposition;
- breath;
- a non-divine spiritual being or sense, according to context.

Contested cases must be documented.

## 18.3 Divine Titles

Capitalize titles when they function as names or recognized divine titles according to standard English practice.

Lexical policy for titles such as *Christ*, *Messiah*, *Son of Man*, and *Lord* belongs to the Contextual Lexical Matrix.

---

# 19. Names and Titles

Use standard English biblical names unless:

- the passage explicitly draws attention to the name’s meaning;
- the name’s meaning affects the argument;
- a different form is required for consistency or clarity.

Make titles understandable.

Terms such as *Christ, Messiah, apostle, Pharisee, Sadducee, synagogue,* and *centurion* may be retained when necessary or historically specific, but should be made intelligible through context or a brief note when the target reader may not understand them.

The Style Guide does not settle every title globally. The Contextual Lexical Matrix records approved contextual patterns.

---

# 20. Money, Measures, Distance, and Time

Translate ancient amounts and measurements into understandable English when doing so improves comprehension without creating false precision.

## 20.1 Money

Translate economic impact when that is the point.

Examples:

- a denarius may be rendered “a day’s wage”;
- a large debt may be rendered by its practical scale rather than an opaque coin name.

Retain the ancient term when:

- the term itself matters;
- the amount is symbolic;
- exact comparison within the passage depends on the original unit;
- conversion would mislead.

## 20.2 Distance and Measurement

Use understandable modern equivalents when:

- visual comprehension matters;
- the conversion can be expressed responsibly;
- symbolic meaning is not lost.

Use approximations when precision would be false.

Preserve the source unit in notes or study data.

## 20.3 Time

Ancient time expressions may become approximate modern times when that helps the reader.

Examples:

- “about nine in the morning”;
- “around noon”;
- “about three in the afternoon.”

Retain the source expression where:

- the literary structure depends on it;
- symbolic meaning is present;
- conversion would create uncertainty or distortion.

## 20.4 International Consideration

The present reading view may use familiar U.S. measurements for its primary audience, but underlying source and study data should preserve original units so later international editions can convert responsibly.

# 21. Old Testament Quotations and Allusions

## 21.1 Direct Quotations

Mark direct Old Testament quotations in the editor or source layer.

Possible source markers include:

```text
{{ot-quote:...}}
```

or:

```text
{{ot-quote-start}}
...
{{ot-quote-end}}
```

Reading-view typography remains an export and design decision.

## 21.2 Allusions and Echoes

Do not mechanically mark all possible allusions.

When an allusion is important, use a brief note such as:

> This wording echoes Isaiah 40:3.

Because identifying an allusion involves judgment, it requires editorial or reviewer approval.

## 21.3 Divine Name in Quotations

When Greek κύριος represents Hebrew YHWH in an Old Testament quotation, preserve that information in source or study metadata.

A source marker may be:

```text
{{divine-name:Lord}}
```

Do not automatically render ordinary New Testament κύριος as *Yahweh* in the body text.

The final visual treatment of the divine name is a separate publication decision.

# 22. Footnotes and Study Information

The default is:

> **Put the meaning in the body text. Use the footnote for information that should not burden the reading text.**

Footnotes should be brief and consistent.

## 22.1 Weighted-Term Note

Use when:

- a theological term is rendered by meaning;
- a retained term needs explanation;
- the reader may benefit from seeing the traditional label.

Example:

> The Greek word often translated “grace” refers here to God’s generous favor.

## 22.2 Alternative-Rendering Note

Use when another rendering is genuinely defensible and reader-relevant.

Example:

> Or, “faithfulness.”

Do not list every technically possible gloss.

## 22.3 Textual-Variant Note

Use when:

- a variant affects meaning;
- readers are likely to notice the difference;
- the variant is important for interpretation.

Do not overload the reading view with minor variants.

## 22.4 Cultural or Historical Note

Use for unfamiliar customs, offices, institutions, geography, money, measures, legal terms, or name meanings.

Keep the note focused on what the reader needs.

## 22.5 Divine-Name Note

Use according to §21.3.

## 22.6 Formal-Source Note

Use “Greek literally…” sparingly.

Use it when:

- the original image matters;
- a metaphor is developed across the passage;
- the literal form helps the reader understand a theological or literary connection.

Do not use it to justify leaving an opaque formal rendering in the body.

## 22.7 Reading View and Study View

The reading view contains clean translation text, natural paragraphing, and minimal necessary notes.

The study view may contain Greek, morphology, lexical information, formal glosses, variant alerts, alignment data, rationale, and decision references.

Detailed interface and export specifications belong in architecture and publishing documents rather than this Style Guide.

---

# 23. Copyright and Process Boundaries

The standalone Copyright Independence Policy governs blind drafting, comparison sets, contamination safeguards, similarity review, logging, and resolution.

This Style Guide adds only two style-facing rules:

1. Existing English translations may calibrate later editorial review but must not source initial wording.
2. Copyright independence must never be achieved by making the English awkward, obscure, inaccurate, or eccentric.

Detailed agent inputs, editor fields, status terms, pass assignments, approval gates, and context-retrieval rules belong in the Translation Packet and architecture documents.

# Appendix A — One-Screen Agent Core

1. **Dynamic expression is the default.** Departure from Greek form needs no defense; the meaning you produce does.
2. **Both-pass rule:** when two options pass the Warrant Test, literal resemblance earns no extra credit. Prefer the clearer or more natural dynamic option.
3. **Aim near sixth-grade readability.** Grade 7 or above triggers review for hidden literalism, but never damage the English to lower a score.
4. **Put meaning in the body.** Do not leave an opaque formal term in the text and place its meaning only in a footnote.
5. **Render church vocabulary by meaning** unless retaining the term clearly earns its place.
6. **Check both directions:** too loose and too literal.
7. **Preserve form that carries meaning.** Reshape form that merely reflects Greek structure.
8. **Clarify what the Greek communicates. Do not add commentary.** Extended unpacking requires independent warrant review.
9. **Add logical connectors only when the relationship is strongly supported and English needs it stated.**
10. **Preserve meaningful ambiguity.** Do not solve it merely for smoothness.
11. **Test the paragraph, not only the sentence.**
12. **Flag oral risks; do not self-certify oral quality or stiffness.**
13. **Draft from Greek first. Compare with modern translations only later.**
14. **Free in form, disciplined in meaning.**
