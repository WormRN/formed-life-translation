# FLT Translation Philosophy Revision Brief

**Project:** Formed Life Translation  
**Document type:** Translation Philosophy Revision Brief  
**System version:** FLT Translation System Version 2.0  
**Status:** Working draft for approval  
**Purpose:** Govern the revision of the FLT Constitution, Style Guide, Contextual Lexical Matrix, agent instructions, checker rules, and Philippians validation process

---

## 1. Purpose of This Revision

The Formed Life Translation was created to communicate the meaning, logic, tone, and theological force of the Greek New Testament in clear, natural English for readers with little or no church background.

Version 1 established the correct mission, but parts of its governing language and review process continued to favor Greek form more strongly than intended. In practice, the system could reward wording that was traceable, defensible, and relatively literal even when it remained stiff, abstract, church-shaped, or difficult for the target reader.

Version 2.0 will correct this imbalance.

FLT will remain fully accountable to the SBLGNT Greek text, but fidelity will be measured primarily by whether the English accurately communicates the meaning of the Greek—not by whether it preserves Greek word order, grammatical form, sentence structure, voice, or lexical correspondence.

The revision does not move FLT away from fidelity. It defines fidelity more accurately for a dynamic-equivalence translation.

---

## 2. The Central Philosophy Shift

### Version 1 tendency

Version 1 often approached translation with this practical question:

> How close can the English remain to the Greek while still being understandable?

That question can produce responsible translations, but it naturally pulls the translation toward formal equivalence. It treats departure from Greek form as a risk that must always be minimized, even when the result is unnatural or unnecessarily difficult English.

### Version 2 governing question

Version 2.0 will ask:

> What clear, natural English most faithfully communicates the full meaning, logic, tone, and theological force of the Greek to the target reader?

A second question will then provide restraint:

> What is the least interpretive departure necessary to accomplish that communication without adding unsupported meaning?

This order matters.

Natural communication of meaning is the steering principle. Minimum interpretive departure is the guardrail.

---

## 3. Revised Translation Position

FLT is a dynamic-equivalence New Testament translation based on the SBLGNT.

Its intended position is:

- closer to the readability, naturalness, and meaning-driven communication associated with the NLT than to the formalness of the CSB;
- more controlled than many highly dynamic translations in its treatment of interpretive additions, ambiguity, recurring theological concepts, and traceability to the Greek;
- written for religion-naive adults and adolescents who read at approximately a sixth-grade level;
- independent in wording and produced directly from the Greek rather than adapted from existing English translations.

FLT does not seek a midpoint between formal and dynamic equivalence. It belongs deliberately on the dynamic-equivalence side of the spectrum.

Its distinctiveness will come from strong semantic controls, documented decisions, contextual lexical policies, independent checking, and transparent study-layer accountability—not from preserving Greek-shaped English in the reading text.

---

## 4. Revised Minimum-Departure Principle

### Governing principle

> Use the least interpretive departure necessary to communicate the full meaning naturally, clearly, and faithfully.

### Explanation

“Minimum departure” refers to departure from the meaning warranted by the source, not merely departure from the visible form of the Greek.

FLT may freely change Greek form when doing so is necessary for natural English. This includes:

- changing word order;
- dividing or combining sentences;
- replacing passive constructions with active ones;
- turning nouns into verbs or verbs into nouns;
- making implicit relationships explicit;
- clarifying pronoun referents;
- rendering idioms by their intended meaning;
- replacing abstract constructions with concrete natural English;
- varying English wording when one fixed equivalent would miscommunicate the Greek in context;
- reorganizing clauses so the reader can follow the argument without reconstructing Greek syntax.

These changes are not translation failures. They are normal tools of dynamic equivalence.

The system should resist a freer rendering only when it:

- adds an interpretation not adequately supported by the Greek and context;
- removes a meaning that the source communicates;
- resolves an important ambiguity that should remain open;
- distorts theological force;
- narrows or expands the author’s claim without sufficient warrant;
- substitutes explanation, commentary, or application for translation.

### Operational summary

Do not ask:

> Did the English preserve the Greek form?

Ask:

> Did the English preserve and communicate the Greek meaning?

## 5. Definition of the Target Reader

The primary FLT reader is a religion-naive adult or adolescent reading at approximately a sixth-grade level.

This reader may:

- have little or no experience reading the Bible;
- misunderstand traditional religious vocabulary;
- recognize common English words while struggling with abstract theological phrasing;
- read competently but lose the thread of long or heavily embedded sentences;
- understand ideas better when relationships are stated clearly;
- encounter the text by listening rather than silent reading;
- be intelligent and mature without possessing academic, biblical, or church vocabulary.

The target reader must not be treated as a child.

Sixth-grade readability does not mean:

- childish tone;
- short, disconnected sentences;
- flattened theology;
- removal of metaphor or emotion;
- oversimplification of complex ideas;
- repetitive explanation of obvious material;
- loss of literary dignity;
- casual or cute language.

The target is mature, natural, accessible English.

The ideal reader response is:

> “This sounds like it was written for an adult, but I can understand it the first time I read or hear it.”

---

## 6. Readability Standard

FLT readability cannot be reduced to a grade-level formula.

Flesch-Kincaid and related measures may identify possible difficulty, but they cannot determine whether a translation is clear, natural, coherent, dignified, or faithful.

Version 2.0 readability review must evaluate at least six dimensions:

### 6.1 Vocabulary accessibility

Are important words understandable to a reader without church training?

Unfamiliar terms may be retained when they are necessary or worth learning, but they must be supported by context, wording, or a brief note.

### 6.2 Sentence comprehension

Can the reader identify who is doing what, to whom, and why without repeatedly rereading the sentence?

### 6.3 Cohesion

Do pronouns, connectors, transitions, repeated ideas, and paragraph relationships allow the reader to follow the passage as a unified piece of communication?

### 6.4 Oral flow

Does the passage sound natural when read aloud? Can a listener follow it without seeing the punctuation or verse numbers?

### 6.5 Paragraph movement

Does the English preserve the movement of the argument, story, exhortation, prayer, or emotional development across the whole paragraph?

### 6.6 Adult naturalness

Does the wording sound like clear contemporary English written for adults rather than simplified school material?

A passage that scores at grade six but sounds choppy, vague, childish, or mechanically simplified has failed the FLT readability standard.

---

## 7. First-Read Clarity

FLT aims for first-read understanding whenever the source meaning permits it.

The central test is:

> Can a first-time reader understand the author’s main communication on the first reading without losing important meaning from the Greek?

First-read clarity does not require every theological implication, cultural background, wordplay, or interpretive possibility to be immediately obvious. Some passages are genuinely difficult.

It does require the translation not to create avoidable difficulty through:

- Greek-shaped syntax;
- unexplained church vocabulary;
- abstract noun clusters;
- unclear referents;
- unnatural passives;
- overloaded sentences;
- formal phrases that ordinary English speakers would not naturally use;
- compressed relationships that English normally states explicitly.

Difficulty belonging to the biblical author may remain.

Difficulty created by the translation should not.

---

## 8. Meaning, Form, and Literary Features

Version 2.0 distinguishes between forms that carry meaningful literary force and forms that are merely features of Greek grammar.

### Preserve when communicatively important

FLT should seek to preserve or recreate:

- emphasis;
- contrast;
- repetition that serves a rhetorical purpose;
- metaphor and imagery;
- emotional intensity;
- irony;
- parallelism;
- argument structure;
- ambiguity when the ambiguity is meaningful;
- shifts in tone;
- memorable or forceful phrasing;
- relationships among clauses and paragraphs.

### Do not preserve merely for formal correspondence

FLT is not required to retain:

- Greek word order;
- grammatical category;
- active or passive voice;
- participial structure;
- clause length;
- sentence boundaries;
- conjunction frequency;
- lexical repetition that sounds accidental or misleading in English;
- singular or plural form when natural English requires a different construction and meaning is preserved.

The goal is functional and communicative correspondence, not formal imitation.

---

## 9. Theological Terms

Theological accuracy will not be protected by automatically preserving traditional English theological vocabulary.

A traditional term may be:

- accurate and understandable;
- accurate but opaque;
- misunderstood by religion-naive readers;
- too narrow for a particular context;
- too broad for a particular context;
- loaded with later theological meanings not foregrounded in the passage.

Version 2.0 will therefore replace the fixed-term glossary model with a Contextual Lexical Matrix.

Each major Greek term or word group should include:

- its relevant semantic range;
- discourse and situational parameters;
- diagnostic questions;
- approved contextual renderings;
- renderings that require caution;
- novice-reader risks;
- risks of theological distortion;
- risks of overinterpretation;
- footnote triggers;
- examples and logged precedents.

Consistency remains valuable, but consistency means applying the same decision process—not forcing the same English word into every context.

## 10. Interpretive Additions and Clarification

All translation involves interpretation. Version 2.0 will distinguish responsible clarification from unsupported addition.

### Responsible clarification

A clarification is permitted when it makes explicit what the Greek and context communicate implicitly and when ordinary English requires that relationship to be stated.

Examples may include:

- identifying an implied subject;
- clarifying a pronoun referent;
- stating an evident logical relationship;
- unpacking an idiom;
- replacing an abstract noun with a natural verbal expression;
- supplying culturally assumed information necessary to understand the sentence.

### Unsupported addition

An addition must be flagged when it:

- imports commentary into the body text;
- selects one disputed theological interpretation without sufficient textual support;
- explains motives or results the source does not communicate;
- inserts devotional application;
- strengthens or softens the author’s claim;
- makes the text more precise than the Greek permits.

The presence of additional English words is not itself evidence of added meaning.

The question is whether additional meaning has been introduced.

---

## 11. Ambiguity

FLT should not preserve ambiguity merely because a Greek form permits several dictionary glosses.

The translator should determine whether the original audience likely understood the expression clearly in context.

Retain or reflect ambiguity when:

- the author appears to use it intentionally;
- multiple senses may be active;
- the evidence does not support choosing one meaning;
- resolving it would materially narrow the theological or rhetorical force.

Clarify when:

- the ambiguity exists mainly for modern readers;
- the context makes the intended meaning sufficiently clear;
- literal English would create a false ambiguity not present for the original audience;
- an English word would be misleading without explanation.

Important unresolved ambiguities should be documented and may receive a note.

## 12. Checker Philosophy

Version 2.0 checkers must evaluate the translation according to FLT’s stated philosophy rather than importing formal-equivalence assumptions.

### The adversarial critic may flag only demonstrable risks

The adversarial critic may object when there is evidence of:

- meaning loss;
- unsupported addition;
- theological distortion;
- misleading simplification;
- unjustified narrowing of ambiguity;
- loss of important discourse or rhetorical force;
- copyright-independence risk.

It may not object merely because the translation changed:

- Greek word order;
- grammatical category;
- voice;
- sentence structure;
- clause boundaries;
- lexical correspondence;
- degree of explicitness required by natural English.

Every objection must identify:

1. the specific Greek meaning allegedly affected;
2. the precise English wording at issue;
3. the nature of the loss, addition, or distortion;
4. the severity of the problem;
5. a proposed correction that remains natural for the target reader.

### Dynamic-equivalence compliance checker

Version 2.0 will add an independent checker that flags wording that remains unnecessarily:

- Greek-shaped;
- formal;
- abstract;
- churchy;
- syntactically compressed;
- unclear on first reading;
- unnatural when spoken;
- dependent on traditional translation language rather than ordinary English.

This checker must not loosen the translation without control. Its recommendations must remain subject to semantic and theological review.

## 13. Copyright Independence

FLT must be translated independently from the Greek.

Modern copyrighted English translations are not drafting sources. They may be consulted only after an independent FLT draft exists.

Such consultation must be logged.

Version 2.0 will establish:

- a formal Copyright Independence Policy;
- draft-stage restrictions on access to modern translations;
- a consultation log;
- exact-phrase and rare-phrase comparison;
- sentence-level and paragraph-level similarity analysis;
- review of distinctive interpretive overlap;
- review of sustained similarity across consecutive verses;
- green, yellow, orange, and red risk categories;
- required human review of flagged results.

Similarity is not proof of copying, and a similarity checker cannot provide a legal guarantee.

Common wording, necessary wording, biblical names, ordinary phrases, and translations strongly constrained by the Greek may legitimately overlap. The review must consider distinctiveness, concentration, sequence, and plausible independent derivation.

---

## 14. Role of the Study Layer

FLT will place natural reader-facing English in the reading text and preserve formal accountability through the study layer.

The reading text exists to communicate Scripture.

The study layer exists to show how the translation relates to the Greek.

The study layer may include:

- Greek text;
- morphology;
- Strong’s identifiers;
- alignment data;
- formal glosses;
- lexical-matrix notes;
- textual variants;
- translation alternatives;
- literal-source notes;
- decision-log links;
- explanations of significant dynamic-equivalence moves.

The reading text should not be forced to carry every feature needed for scholarly traceability.

---

## 15. Human Editorial Authority

Blind worker agents, checker agents, deterministic tests, and decision briefs support the translation process, but they do not determine the final text.

Agents will not communicate directly with one another. A software harness will route packets, preserve blindness, collect structured outputs, classify disputes, and prepare the evidence required for a decision brief.

The boss or secretary agent may synthesize arguments but may not silently decide contested translation questions.

The human editor remains the final authority for:

- translation wording;
- theological judgment;
- lexical precedents;
- acceptance or rejection of checker findings;
- copyright-risk review;
- footnotes;
- governing-document revisions;
- release status.

No agent vote overrides human judgment.

---

## 16. Version 2.0 Validation

Philippians will serve as the engine-validation book.

Philippians 1:1–11 will be the first pilot passage.

Earlier Philippians decisions will be treated as provisional evidence, not binding precedent. They must be retested under Version 2.0 because they were produced under a system whose governing philosophy and checker incentives are now being revised.

The pilot should test whether the new system:

- produces more natural and readable English;
- retains the meaning and theological force of the Greek;
- distinguishes meaning fidelity from form preservation;
- resists unsupported interpretive additions;
- identifies churchy and Greek-shaped wording;
- uses the Contextual Lexical Matrix effectively;
- produces useful, non-duplicative checker reports;
- preserves agent blindness;
- produces a decision brief a human editor can realistically use;
- identifies possible copyright similarity without treating all overlap as infringement.

The pilot is a system test, not merely a translation contest.

---

## 17. Success Criteria for the Philosophy Revision

The revision succeeds when:

1. Worker agents no longer assume that the most literal defensible wording is automatically the safest wording.
2. Checker agents do not penalize legitimate changes of form.
3. Natural sixth-grade adult English is treated as a core translation requirement.
4. The target reader can follow the paragraph on first reading or hearing.
5. The English communicates the propositions, relationships, tone, and theological force of the Greek.
6. Unsupported explanation is identified and removed.
7. Important ambiguity is preserved or documented.
8. Major Greek terms are handled contextually and consistently through explicit decision rules.
9. Copyright independence is built into the workflow from the first draft onward.
10. The human editor receives focused evidence and real tradeoffs rather than competing preferences for literalness.
11. The final translation sounds like coherent contemporary English rather than simplified Greek.
12. Every accepted rendering remains independently defensible from the source text and FLT’s governing documents.

## 19. Governing Summary

FLT Version 2.0 will translate the Greek New Testament into clear, natural, mature English for religion-naive readers.

It will preserve meaning rather than imitate form.

It will depart freely from Greek structure when natural English requires it, but it will not depart freely from what the Greek communicates.

It will value first-read clarity without flattening theology.

It will use sixth-grade accessibility without sounding childish.

It will control interpretation through semantic review, contextual lexical policies, independent checkers, structured disagreement, transparent documentation, and final human judgment.

Its guiding question will be:

> What clear and natural English most faithfully communicates everything this passage is doing for the reader?

Its controlling guardrail will be:

> Do not add, remove, narrow, or distort meaning beyond what the Greek and its context support.
