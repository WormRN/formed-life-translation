# The Formed Life Translation

## Copyright Independence Policy — Version 2.0

> **Every FLT rendering must be reached independently from the Greek, justified independently from the Greek, and reviewed for distinctive overlap with existing English translations. Similarity may require investigation, but similarity alone neither proves copying nor guarantees legal safety.**

## 1. Purpose

The Formed Life Translation is intended to be an original English translation of the Greek New Testament.

It must not be created by adapting, revising, combining, or paraphrasing existing English Bible translations. Its wording must arise from:

- the Greek source text;
- textual and linguistic evidence;
- the FLT Constitution;
- the Style Guide;
- the Contextual Lexical Matrix;
- independently developed editorial judgment.

Copyright law distinguishes underlying ideas and facts from the particular expression used to communicate them. Translations may contain protectable original expression even though they communicate the same source meaning.

This policy therefore protects both:

- the independence of the translation process;
- the originality of the resulting English expression.

It is a process safeguard, not a legal guarantee.

## 2. The Independent-Path Requirement

Every proposed FLT rendering must have a documented or explainable path:

> **Greek text → contextual meaning → independent English expression**

A rendering fails the independence requirement when its actual path is:

> **existing English translation → alteration, combination, or paraphrase → FLT wording**

The translator or reviewing agent must be able to explain why the Greek and its context support the FLT wording without relying on another translation’s phrasing as the justification.

A wording choice is not made independent merely by changing a few words after borrowing another translation’s structure or interpretive solution.

The governing question is:

> **Would this FLT rendering plausibly exist in substantially this form if the compared modern translations had not been opened?**

## 3. Order of Operations

Modern English translations may be consulted only after an independent FLT draft exists.

The required order is:

1. Analyze the Greek text and context.
2. Produce the independent FLT draft.
3. Record the draft before comparison.
4. Consult modern translations only for review.
5. Log the translations consulted and the purpose of consultation.
6. Run similarity checks.
7. Review flagged similarities without allowing the comparison text to become the new drafting source.

Modern translations may be used after drafting to:

- identify interpretive options;
- expose a meaning the FLT draft may have missed;
- test whether ambiguity has been handled responsibly;
- compare readability and oral flow;
- identify possible accidental similarity.

They may not be used to:

- supply the first English draft;
- provide a sentence template;
- furnish an elegant phrase for adoption;
- serve as the baseline that FLT edits;
- combine favored phrases from several translations;
- generate “different enough” wording through superficial synonym replacement.

Consultation tests the independent draft. It does not create it.

## 4. Blind Drafting Requirements

Worker agents responsible for initial translation must not receive copyrighted English translations of the passage in the active drafting context.

The software harness must preserve this blindness.

Initial worker inputs may include:

- the Greek text;
- morphology and lexical data;
- textual-context information;
- approved FLT governing documents;
- relevant Contextual Lexical Matrix entries;
- prior FLT decisions that have already passed the required similarity review.

The initial packet must not include:

- NLT, NIV, CSB, ESV, NASB, CEV, GNT, *The Message*, or other copyrighted translation text;
- notes that disclose their distinctive wording;
- a composite draft derived from them;
- unreviewed prior FLT wording that may carry unlogged overlap.

Because an AI model may already contain translation language within its training data, “blind” means comparison texts are withheld from the active prompt and workflow. It does not claim that the model has never encountered them.

Comparison material may be introduced only during later copyright and interpretive-review stages.

## 5. AI-Specific Risk

AI-generated wording must not be presumed independent merely because the model was not shown a modern translation in the current prompt.

Models may reproduce familiar or distinctive wording encountered in training data. Agreement among several models also does not establish independence, because multiple models may echo the same widely represented source.

Therefore:

- every AI draft is provisional;
- model confidence does not establish originality;
- agreement between agents does not establish originality;
- an explanation written after generation does not by itself prove an independent path;
- AI-produced text must undergo the same or stronger similarity review as human-produced text;
- final wording requires human editorial adoption and documented judgment.

FLT must preserve substantial human control over selection, revision, arrangement, and final wording.

## 6. Necessary and Incidental Overlap

Some overlap among Bible translations is unavoidable.

This may include:

- names and titles;
- common vocabulary;
- standard theological terms;
- short ordinary phrases;
- wording strongly constrained by the Greek;
- fixed quotations or familiar expressions;
- sentences for which natural English offers few responsible alternatives.

Examples such as “Jesus wept” or “in the beginning” should not be replaced with inferior English merely to appear original.

A shared phrase may remain when:

1. it was independently reached;
2. it follows naturally from the Greek;
3. it is common, conventional, or strongly constrained;
4. changing it would reduce accuracy, naturalness, or clarity;
5. the surrounding passage does not show a wider pattern of derivative similarity.

Difference is not the goal. Independence is.

## 7. Distinctive and Derivative Overlap

Similarity becomes more concerning when the shared language reflects discretionary creative choices rather than inevitable translation.

Indicators include:

- an unusual multiword phrase;
- the same uncommon vocabulary choice;
- the same reordering of a difficult sentence;
- the same unpacking of an idiom;
- the same clarification of an ambiguity;
- the same supplied explanatory content;
- the same rhythm or clause sequence;
- several shared choices concentrated within one verse or paragraph;
- sustained similarity across consecutive verses;
- agreement with one modern version where other natural solutions were readily available.

A distinctive match does not automatically prove copying. It triggers investigation.

The greater the interpretive and expressive freedom involved in a passage, the more carefully distinctive overlap must be reviewed.

## 8. Similarity Review

The final independent draft must be compared against a defined set of modern copyrighted translations.

Version 2.0 deliberately expands the active comparison set beyond the earlier NLT–NIV–CSB benchmark to include the ESV, NASB, CEV, GNT, and *The Message*.

The active comparison set is:

- NLT;
- NIV;
- CSB;
- ESV;
- NASB;
- CEV;
- GNT;
- *The Message*.

The wider set is used only after independent drafting and exists to detect distinctive overlap across a broader range of modern English translations.

Version 2.0 supersedes the earlier fixed four-word rule. No single word-count threshold determines independence. Exact-match length remains one diagnostic signal among several, alongside rarity, distinctiveness, structural similarity, interpretive overlap, concentration, and sustained similarity.

Similarity review should flag:

- exact phrase overlap;
- rare or distinctive shared phrases;
- sentence-level similarity;
- paragraph-level similarity;
- distinctive interpretive overlap;
- shared additions or clarifications;
- similar clause order and restructuring;
- sustained similarity across consecutive verses;
- unusually high concentration of overlap with one translation.

Mechanical matching is evidence for review, not a verdict.

## 9. Risk Categories

### Green — Ordinary or constrained overlap

Examples:

- common words and names;
- short conventional expressions;
- wording strongly dictated by the Greek;
- isolated similarity with no broader pattern.

**Action:** No change required. Logging is optional.

### Yellow — Noticeable but plausibly independent

Examples:

- a longer shared phrase made mostly of common language;
- one similar sentence structure;
- an isolated shared interpretive choice.

**Action:** Confirm the independent path and briefly document the judgment.

### Orange — Distinctive or concentrated similarity

Examples:

- a rare shared phrase;
- the same creative restructuring;
- several similarities within one sentence or paragraph;
- distinctive overlap with one dynamic translation.

**Action:** Mandatory human review. Return to the Greek and develop independent alternatives. Retain the original only when its independence and superiority can be clearly documented.

### Red — Probable derivation or sustained dependence

Examples:

- repeated distinctive similarity across consecutive verses;
- wording obviously revised from another translation;
- shared sentence structures and interpretive additions throughout a paragraph;
- direct copying or close paraphrase.

**Action:** Reject the affected draft. Re-translate from the Greek in a clean, comparison-blind process and repeat the review.

## 10. Handling a Flagged Similarity

When a similarity is flagged:

1. **Return to the recorded independent draft.** Confirm that it existed before comparison.
2. **Identify the shared expression.** Determine whether the similarity concerns common wording, constrained wording, or a distinctive creative solution.
3. **Reconstruct the independent path.** Explain how the Greek text and context produced the FLT wording.
4. **Examine the wider pattern.** Look beyond the isolated phrase to the sentence, paragraph, and surrounding verses.
5. **Generate alternatives from the Greek.** Do not merely swap synonyms into the compared translation’s structure.
6. **Compare quality.** Determine whether an independent alternative is equally or more accurate, clear, natural, and consistent with FLT.
7. **Make a human decision.** Retain, revise, or reject the wording.
8. **Log yellow, orange, and red decisions.**

A checker agent may flag the issue and suggest areas for reconsideration. It must not automatically rewrite the verse merely to break a match.

## 11. Do Not Degrade the Translation to Avoid Similarity

Copyright review must not produce awkward, inaccurate, obscure, or unnatural English merely to eliminate shared wording.

Do not:

- replace the best ordinary word with an unusual synonym;
- damage sentence rhythm;
- obscure the meaning;
- remove needed connectors;
- invent novel expressions for their own sake;
- alter theology;
- produce wording that sounds unnatural solely to reduce a similarity score.

When overlap is necessary, independently reached, and non-distinctive, retain the better translation and document the reason when appropriate.

The policy requires independent expression, not conspicuous novelty.

## 12. Public-Domain and Openly Licensed Texts

Public-domain or openly licensed translations may not present the same copyright restrictions as protected modern translations, but they must not replace the Greek as FLT’s source.

They may be consulted under the same basic order:

1. independent FLT draft first;
2. consultation second;
3. no adaptation of their wording as the FLT drafting method.

The concern is not only legal permission. FLT also requires methodological independence and a consistent contemporary voice.

## 13. Third-Party Source and License Register

Every third-party source text, dataset, translation, lexicon, software library, model, or tool incorporated materially into the FLT workflow must have the following recorded:

- source and provider;
- version or edition where applicable;
- licensing status;
- permitted use;
- attribution requirements;
- relevant restrictions;
- date reviewed.

This requirement includes, but is not limited to:

- SBLGNT;
- parsed Greek datasets;
- lexicons;
- morphology and tagging resources;
- comparison translations;
- software dependencies;
- model providers;
- similarity tools.

## 14. Consultation Log

Every formal comparison with modern translations must record:

- passage;
- date;
- FLT draft version or commit;
- translations consulted;
- purpose of consultation;
- significant similarities found;
- risk category;
- human decision;
- revisions made;
- reviewer.

This establishes that comparison occurred after the independent draft and creates an auditable process history.

## 15. Limitations

No similarity test can:

- determine legal infringement by itself;
- prove that wording was independently created;
- guarantee that a text is free from copyright risk;
- replace qualified legal review before publication;
- convert a derivative process into an independent one merely because no long exact match was found.

The checker provides evidence and risk signals. The human editor makes the editorial decision. Legal counsel should review the publication and licensing plan before public release.

## 16. Summary Rule

> **Draft from the Greek first. Record the independent wording before opening modern translations. Consult other translations only to test the work, never to source it. Review exact, distinctive, structural, interpretive, and sustained similarity. Preserve natural wording when overlap is inevitable and independently reached. Reject wording that depends on another translation’s creative expression. Never make the English worse merely to make it look different.**
