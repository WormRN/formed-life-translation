# The Formed Life Translation

## Constitution — Version 2.0

> **The Formed Life Translation renders the meaning of the Greek New Testament into clear, natural, dignified English for readers who bring little or no religious background to the text. It aims to make Scripture genuinely understandable on a first reading or hearing without reducing what Scripture means.**

This Constitution is the highest governing document of the Formed Life Translation project. Every other governing document implements this Constitution and remains subordinate to it.

Version 2.0 rests on a materially different translation philosophy from Version 1. It supersedes the earlier Constitution, which remains archived as a historical record.

---

# Part I — Purpose and Identity

## 1. What FLT Is

The Formed Life Translation, or FLT, is an original English translation of the Greek New Testament intended for free public use.

It is written primarily for religion-naive adults and older adolescents: intelligent readers who can follow stories, arguments, emotions, and difficult ideas but who read English at approximately a sixth-grade level and do not already understand the language and assumptions of church culture.

FLT exists to allow these readers to encounter the New Testament in language they can understand without first learning a specialized religious vocabulary.

## 2. What FLT Is Not

FLT is not:

- a revision of an existing English translation;
- a compilation of preferred readings from several translations;
- a devotional paraphrase;
- a children’s Bible;
- a commentary disguised as a translation;
- a simplified substitute for the full theological content of Scripture.

FLT does not lower, sanitize, or domesticate the message of Scripture. It removes avoidable barriers created by unfamiliar vocabulary, Greek-shaped syntax, compressed logic, and unnatural English.

## 3. Translation Position

FLT is a dynamic-equivalence translation positioned near the NLT side of the translation spectrum: generally freer than the CSB and more restrained than *The Message*.

This comparison describes its intended degree of naturalness. Existing translations do not serve as its source, template, or drafting baseline.

# Part II — Governing Translation Commitments

## 4. Meaning Governs Form

FLT translates the meaning of the Greek text—its logic, tone, emphasis, imagery, rhetorical force, and theological weight—into the clearest natural English available to the target reader.

Greek form should be preserved when it communicates naturally and carries meaningful literary or rhetorical force. It should be reshaped when preserving it would create unclear, misleading, overly formal, abstract, or unnatural English.

## 5. First-Read Oral Clarity

The reader should ordinarily be able to understand the author’s main communication on the first reading or hearing without specialized religious knowledge.

FLT must be tested as spoken language as well as written language. A rendering that appears clear on the page but becomes confusing when heard has not fully passed.

## 6. Accountability to the Source

Every significant meaning carried into the English must be responsibly defensible from the Greek text and its literary, linguistic, historical, and theological context.

Clarity cannot justify:

- unsupported addition;
- meaning loss;
- theological distortion;
- unwarranted certainty;
- ornamental paraphrase;
- commentary inserted into the reading text.

Faithfulness is measured neither by formal similarity to Greek nor by natural English alone. FLT requires both faithful meaning and effective communication.

# Part III — The Strategic Departure Principle

> **Depart freely from Greek form whenever clear, natural English requires it. Every meaning carried into the English must remain accountable to the Greek text and its context.**

## 7. Dynamic Expression Is the Normal Posture

Reordering, restructuring, clarifying, unpacking, and changing grammatical form are ordinary acts of translation, not exceptional permissions.

Translators may, when English requires it:

- reorder words, clauses, and sentences;
- divide or combine sentences;
- change nouns into verbs or verbs into nouns;
- change passive constructions into active ones;
- clarify implied subjects, objects, referents, and relationships;
- translate idioms according to their intended meaning;
- replace abstract constructions with natural verbal language;
- vary the rendering of a Greek term when context requires it;
- recreate emphasis, contrast, repetition, and rhetorical force through natural English forms.

The measure of faithfulness is not how closely the English resembles Greek structure. It is how fully and accurately the English communicates the meaning, logic, tone, emphasis, and rhetorical force of the Greek.

## 8. Semantic Discipline Defines the Boundary

FLT does not retain the older principle of minimum departure as a presumption in favor of literalness. Version 2.0 replaces it with semantic discipline:

> **Travel as far from Greek form as clear communication requires, but do not carry the English beyond what the Greek text and its context warrant.**

Semantic discipline restrains:

- **meaning drift:** altering, weakening, strengthening, narrowing, or expanding the author’s meaning;
- **unsupported addition:** introducing explanations, motives, applications, or doctrinal conclusions the source does not adequately support;
- **meaning loss:** omitting or flattening an important proposition, relationship, contrast, image, emphasis, tone, or theological claim;
- **unwarranted certainty:** resolving an important ambiguity without sufficient evidence;
- **loss of textual anchors:** unpacking a term, metaphor, repetition, or argument connection so completely that its function can no longer be traced;
- **ornamental paraphrase:** adding color, personality, drama, humor, or emotional force beyond what the source communicates;
- **commentary in the text:** replacing translation with explanation better suited to a footnote or study layer.

FLT may be as expressive as the Greek. It must not become more expressive than the Greek.

## 9. Formal Departure Is Free; Interpretive Content Is Accountable

Departure from Greek structure requires no special defense.

The meaning expressed through that departure always does.

Formal distance is not evidence of unfaithfulness. Natural English may differ substantially from Greek form while communicating the source more accurately than a close structural rendering.

## 10. Dynamic Expression Breaks the Tie

When a formal rendering and a dynamic rendering communicate the Greek meaning equally faithfully, FLT prefers the dynamic rendering that produces clearer, more natural English.

Literalness is one possible means of fidelity. It is not the definition of fidelity and is not the tiebreaker.

A traditional rendering receives no preference merely because it is familiar or common in existing translations.

## 11. Form Preserved When It Carries Meaning

FLT does not treat form as disposable.

Formal features should be preserved or functionally recreated when they carry communicative force, including:

- deliberate repetition;
- metaphor and imagery;
- contrast and emphasis;
- parallelism;
- irony;
- emotional intensity;
- argument structure;
- shifts in tone;
- memorable phrasing;
- meaningful ambiguity;
- connections across a paragraph or book.

The translator asks not only whether a form exists, but what that form accomplishes. When its effect cannot be carried naturally through the same form, FLT recreates the effect through another English form rather than imitating the Greek mechanically.

# Part IV — The Sixth-Grade Adult Reader Standard

> **FLT is written for an intelligent adult or older adolescent who lacks religious background and reads at approximately a sixth-grade level. The reading level describes the prose, not the reader’s mind. Never write down to this person.**

## 12. The Target Reader

The target reader can:

- follow a story and a sustained line of thought;
- understand moral seriousness and emotional complexity;
- grasp difficult ideas when they are expressed clearly;
- recognize grief, loyalty, betrayal, injustice, hope, shame, love, and responsibility.

The reader may nevertheless struggle with:

- long or heavily nested sentences;
- unclear pronouns;
- abstract noun clusters;
- compressed logical relationships;
- rare vocabulary;
- traditional religious terminology;
- assumptions drawn from church culture.

FLT simplifies language while respecting intelligence.

## 13. What Sixth-Grade Language Means

Sixth-grade accessibility normally involves:

- common contemporary English;
- short-to-moderate sentence length;
- mostly direct sentence structure;
- limited clause nesting;
- clear subjects and referents;
- modifiers placed near what they modify;
- logical relationships made clear;
- information paced in manageable units;
- coherent paragraph movement.

These are working tendencies, not mechanical rules. Naturalness, meaning, rhythm, and cohesion must not be sacrificed to force every sentence into the same structure.

## 14. What Sixth-Grade Language Does Not Mean

It does not mean:

- childish;
- patronizing;
- choppy;
- sanitized;
- emotionally flat;
- conceptually shallow;
- vague;
- lifeless;
- stripped of literary force;
- artificially reduced to short words.

Simple but disconnected or condescending prose fails the target reader as surely as dense and impenetrable prose does.

## 15. Religion-Naive Vocabulary

FLT assumes the reader does not already understand traditional church terminology.

Words such as *justification, sanctification, propitiation, redemption,* and specialized uses of *righteousness, flesh, grace, gospel, saints, elect,* or *glory* must not be relied upon as self-explanatory labels.

This does not remove the underlying concepts. It requires their meaning to reach the reader through understandable English, context, a footnote, or the study layer.

Some important theological words may be retained when they are understandable, necessary, or worth teaching. When retained, they must be made intelligible.

Recurring terminology is governed by the Contextual Lexical Matrix rather than by rigid one-word glosses.

## 16. Adult Dignity and Naturalness

FLT should sound like an intelligent adult speaking honestly to another adult.

Its language should be:

- mature but accessible;
- emotionally serious;
- natural in contemporary English;
- free from juvenile slang;
- free from academic stiffness;
- respectful of the reader’s intelligence;
- suitable for public oral reading.

FLT does not over-explain, moralize, or prompt the reader toward a preferred emotional reaction.

The governing test is:

> **Would a thoughtful adult who reads at this level feel respected by this sentence or managed by it?**

## 17. Oral Comprehension

Every rendering must be tested for forward-moving oral comprehension.

The listener should not need to see punctuation, reread a clause, or search backward for a pronoun’s referent in order to understand the main communication.

FLT should avoid:

- garden-path constructions;
- unclear referents;
- overloaded clauses;
- confusing sound patterns;
- awkward cadence;
- misplaced emphasis;
- sentences whose logic becomes clear only at the end.

## 18. Theological Complexity

The reader’s reading level limits sentence construction, not theological depth.

FLT preserves:

- complex arguments;
- hard teachings;
- genuine paradox;
- unresolved tension;
- theological depth;
- moral seriousness;
- emotional intensity;
- meaningful ambiguity.

Difficulty belonging to the biblical text must remain. Avoidable difficulty created by the translation must be removed.

A reader may find the idea demanding. The translation should not make the idea harder by expressing it in unnecessarily difficult prose.

## 19. Readability Formulas

Readability formulas are diagnostic tools, not governing authorities.

They may flag passages that deserve review because of:

- sentence length;
- dense syntax;
- clause stacking;
- clusters of difficult vocabulary.

They must never be used to justify:

- breaking coherent sentences into fragments;
- deleting logical connectors;
- replacing precise words with vague ones;
- selecting childish language;
- flattening rhythm;
- weakening meaning;
- damaging paragraph cohesion.

When a numerical score conflicts with natural, dignified, coherent English, qualitative judgment governs.

# Part V — Source Text and Textual Policy

## 20. Governing Base Text

The SBLGNT is the governing base text of FLT.

Translation proceeds from the Greek text using responsible grammatical, lexical, discourse, historical, and contextual analysis.

No existing English translation, lexical gloss, AI output, or downstream FLT document overrides the Greek source.

## 21. Textual Variants

FLT normally translates the SBLGNT reading.

When a significant textual variant affects meaning or is important for the reader’s understanding, it may be documented in a footnote or study layer.

The body text should not silently incorporate a different reading merely because it is familiar from another translation.

Significant textual decisions must be recorded.

## 22. Third-Party Sources

Every third-party source text, dataset, lexicon, software dependency, model, or tool used materially in the FLT process must have its source, licensing status, permitted use, and relevant restrictions recorded in the appropriate project register.

# Part VI — Semantic and Theological Safeguards

## 23. Standing Safeguards

No FLT rendering may:

- add meaning the source does not adequately support;
- remove or flatten meaningful content;
- alter theology to fit the translator’s preferred system;
- resolve important ambiguity without sufficient warrant;
- replace translation with commentary;
- erase meaningful literary or rhetorical features;
- sacrifice the author’s logic, tone, emphasis, or force for readability.

These are boundary conditions of faithful translation, not optional stylistic preferences.

## 24. Theological Vocabulary

FLT keeps a theological term when the target reader can understand it and when it communicates accurately.

It translates an opaque term by its contextual meaning when the label alone would not communicate adequately.

Where fuller explanation is needed, it belongs in a footnote or study layer rather than being inserted into the reading text as hidden commentary.

A single Greek term may receive different English renderings where its contextual function differs. The Contextual Lexical Matrix records:

- semantic range;
- contextual considerations;
- approved renderings;
- cautions;
- important precedents.

Consistency is accountability to meaning, not automatic repetition of the same English word.

# Part VII — Copyright Independence

## 25. Constitutional Requirements

FLT must be independently translated from the Greek and must not be adapted from copyrighted English translations.

Three requirements are constitutional:

1. **Blind independent drafting:** Initial drafts are produced from the Greek and approved FLT governing materials without copyrighted translations of the passage being supplied in the drafting context. Because an AI model may already contain translation language within its training data, “blind” means comparison texts are withheld from the active prompt and workflow; it does not claim that the model has never encountered them.
2. **Comparison after drafting:** Modern translations may be consulted only after the independent draft has been recorded.
3. **Independence without degradation:** FLT must not borrow another translation’s distinctive creative expression, but neither should its English be made awkward, inaccurate, or unnatural merely to look different.

The detailed comparison set, similarity procedures, risk categories, consultation records, and resolution process are governed by the standalone Copyright Independence Policy.

That policy is a process safeguard and does not by itself guarantee freedom from legal risk. Publication and licensing decisions remain subject to appropriate human and legal review.

# Part VIII — Human and AI Authority

## 26. AI Roles

AI agents may:

- draft proposed renderings;
- analyze Greek grammar and vocabulary;
- evaluate readability;
- critique meaning and naturalness;
- compare alternative renderings;
- identify possible theological, discourse, and copyright risks;
- produce structured reports and decision briefs.

Their outputs are proposals and evidence, not final decisions.

## 27. AI Limitations

AI confidence does not establish correctness.

Agreement among several models does not establish correctness, faithfulness, or originality. Several models may share the same training bias or reproduce the same error.

AI explanations may also rationalize wording after it has been produced. Every proposed rendering must therefore be evaluated independently against the Greek and the governing FLT standards.

## 28. Human Editorial Authority

Final editorial authority rests with the designated human editor.

No wording becomes part of FLT merely because an agent proposed it, several agents preferred it, or a checker approved it.

The human editor:

- adopts, revises, or rejects wording;
- resolves substantive disputes;
- approves exceptions;
- identifies binding precedents;
- records significant decisions;
- authorizes changes to governing documents.

AI assists the judgment. It does not replace it.

# Part IX — Quality Control

## 29. The Two Required Tests

Every rendering must pass both tests.

### Communication Test

> Does this wording communicate the passage clearly, naturally, coherently, and appropriately to the target reader?

### Warrant Test

> Can every significant meaning expressed in the English be responsibly defended from the Greek text and its context?

A rendering that passes only the warrant test may remain accurate but unreadable.

A rendering that passes only the communication test may be clear but unsupported.

FLT requires both.

## 30. Required Review Lenses

Every translation unit must be evaluated through the following review lenses before final approval. The Translation Packet and agent rules determine which lenses are combined, which require separate reviewers, and which findings operate as blocking gates.

The translation process must evaluate:

- first-read comprehension;
- oral flow;
- adult naturalness;
- religion-naive vocabulary;
- sentence and paragraph cohesion;
- discourse and argument structure;
- theological faithfulness;
- meaningful ambiguity;
- consistency with the Contextual Lexical Matrix;
- copyright independence;
- continuity with prior binding decisions.

The paragraph, not merely the sentence, is the primary unit of clarity.

## 30.1 Required Pre-Audit Oral-English Smoothing Gate

Every provisional human-selected translation unit must undergo a source-aware Oral-English Smoothing Pass before its exact wording is sealed for semantic audit.

The gate has four ordered stages:

1. **Listener-only diagnosis.** Reviewers receive only the English and identify concrete one-hearing obstacles. They may not consult the Greek, infer missing source wording, or rewrite the text.
2. **Source-aware proposals.** Reviewers receive the selected English, anonymous listener reports, Greek source data, governing rules, and relevant Matrix entries. They may propose only targeted repairs to identified comprehension or oral-flow problems.
3. **Human approval of exact wording.** Every proposed change must be logged, and no change becomes FLT wording until the human editor approves the exact text.
4. **Exact-candidate audit.** The human-approved wording must pass the Warrant Test and Communication Test through the required semantic-floor audit.

Smoothing is controlled comprehension repair, not general beautification. It may split, combine, reorder, or unpack English; bring the main assertion forward; convert abstract noun phrases into natural verbs; vary English transitions; or move secondary explanation into notes. It must preserve semantic propositions, logical relationships, rhetorical force, meaningful ambiguity, agency and force, key-term continuity, and source traceability.

The smallest faithful repair is preferred. A proposal that silently changes wording, settles a source ambiguity for smoothness, weakens deliberate rhetoric, or changes certainty, agency, or logic must be rejected.

If chapter-level oral review later changes accepted wording, the changed exact candidate returns through this gate and receives a fresh audit of the affected unit.

# Part X — Governance

## 31. Governing Hierarchy

The governing hierarchy is:

> **Constitution → Standalone Policies → Style Guide → Contextual Lexical Matrix → Translation Packet and Agent Rules → Decision Log**

The Constitution defines the identity, philosophy, authority, and boundaries of FLT.

Standalone policies govern specialized areas such as copyright independence, source provenance, licensing, and other matters requiring dedicated controls.

The Style Guide converts constitutional principles into repeatable editorial practice.

The Contextual Lexical Matrix governs recurring lexical and conceptual decisions.

Translation Packets and agent rules govern individual drafting, checking, and review cycles.

The Decision Log records adopted decisions and precedents. It cannot override any governing document above it.

## 32. Conflict Resolution

When documents conflict:

1. The Constitution governs all other documents.
2. A specialized policy governs its stated subject when consistent with the Constitution.
3. The Style Guide governs ordinary editorial practice.
4. The Contextual Lexical Matrix governs approved lexical patterns.
5. Translation Packets and agent rules govern the active workflow.
6. The Decision Log supplies precedent only where higher documents permit more than one responsible option.

When all downstream documents are silent on a question, the translator or agent must move upward through the governing hierarchy and apply the nearest relevant principle. If the Constitution and other governing documents do not yield a responsible answer, the matter must be escalated to the human editor rather than resolved through an invented local rule.

## 33. Amendment

Working documents may be revised without amending this Constitution when their revisions remain consistent with it.

A change to any of the following requires a new constitutional version:

- the purpose or intended audience of FLT;
- its position as a dynamic-equivalence translation;
- the Strategic Departure Principle;
- the Sixth-Grade Adult Reader Standard;
- the governing source text;
- the final authority of the human editor;
- the constitutional requirements of copyright independence.
