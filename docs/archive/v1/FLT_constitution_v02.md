# FLT — Constitution (v02)

**Project:** Formed Life Translation (FLT)  
**Document type:** Constitution — the foundational, slow-changing layer of the project  
**Status:** v02 working constitution  
**Created:** 2026-06-12  
**Supersedes:** `FLT_constitution_v01.md`  

> **What this document is.** The constitution fixes the things that should almost never change: mission, authority, source-text policy, translation philosophy, editorial control, public-use intent, and non-negotiable safeguards.
>
> **What this document is not.** It is not the full Style Guide, the full Glossary, the full Translation Packet Template, or the Decision Log. Those faster-moving documents are governed by this constitution but may be revised more often.

---

## 1. Mission

The Formed Life Translation is a clear, natural, dynamic-equivalence translation of the New Testament from the Greek. It exists so that the meaning, logic, tone, and theological force of the text can be understood by readers with little or no church background.

Its purpose is comprehension, Scripture engagement, discipleship, and free digital access — not the display of translation technique.

Working aim:

> Translate the meaning of the Greek New Testament into clear contemporary English that can be understood by new believers, ordinary readers, curious outsiders, and people unfamiliar with church vocabulary, while remaining accountable to the Greek source text through documented decisions and transparent study apparatus.

---

## 2. Audience

The primary reader is religion-naive and Bible-illiterate: a new believer, curious outsider, returning churchgoer, or ordinary reader unfamiliar with traditional church vocabulary.

FLT assumes no prior knowledge of theological terms. Unexplained “Bible words” are treated as possible barriers. They are retained only when they are clear enough, essential enough, or important enough for the reader to learn. When such terms are retained, the surrounding context, footnote, or glossary should help the reader.

The intended reader should regularly feel:

> “I can understand this. I can keep reading.”

---

## 3. Document Hierarchy

FLT is governed by several layers. The slower layer controls the faster layer.

1. **Constitution** — mission, authority, source-text policy, translation philosophy, non-negotiable rules.
2. **Style Guide** — voice, formatting, reading level, punctuation, paragraphing, footnotes, typography, capitalization, view-specific presentation.
3. **Glossary / Key-Term Policy** — recurring Greek terms, semantic ranges, default renderings, exceptions, and decision history.
4. **Translation Packet Template** — the standard working form for chapter and unit review.
5. **Decision Log** — the record of significant decisions and why they were made.
6. **Chapter Editor Files** — verse-by-verse working files with Greek, gloss, draft, editor text, notes, footnotes, and variant alerts.
7. **Reading View** — clean reader-facing text.
8. **Study / Interlinear View** — reverse interlinear, formal gloss, Strong’s spine, notes, and other accountability tools.

If a conflict appears, the Constitution governs first, then the Style Guide, then the Glossary, then the packet or chapter file.

---

## 4. Source-Text Policy

The FLT production base is the **SBL Greek New Testament (SBLGNT)**, used in a parsed form that carries, per word, the Greek form, lemma, Strong’s number, and morphological parse.

The SBLGNT source file is immutable. It is never edited directly.

The **NA28** is used as the primary scholarly control text by the human editor, but the NA28 text and apparatus are not copied, encoded, uploaded, distributed, or incorporated as the project’s digital source base unless explicit permission is obtained from the rights holder.

---

## 5. Source Hierarchy

FLT uses the following hierarchy:

1. **Production Base:** SBLGNT parsed text.
2. **Primary Control Text:** NA28, consulted manually by the human editor.
3. **Variant Alert Sources:** SBLGNT apparatus, NA28 comparison, Robinson-Pierpont/Byzantine comparison, and known KJV/Textus-Receptus-family differences when reader-visible.
4. **Secondary Helps:** lexicons, grammars, textual commentaries, OT source texts, citation lists, major translation notes, and scholarly discussion.
5. **Excluded as Source Text:** copied NA28 digital text, copyrighted modern English translation wording, and any proprietary text that cannot be used in the project’s open digital workflow.

The editor may decide to follow SBLGNT, NA28, or another well-supported reading in a specific location, but any departure from the SBLGNT production base must be documented in the Decision Log when it affects translation.

---

## 6. Textual Variant Alert Policy

The editorial process must alert the human editor when the SBLGNT apparatus or comparison data indicates a potentially meaningful difference between SBLGNT and NA28, Robinson-Pierpont/Byzantine, or a known KJV/Textus-Receptus-family reading.

Variant alerts are **review triggers**, not automatic decisions. They exist to tell the editor, “check here.”

To prevent overload, variant alerts are ranked by translation impact:

| Level | Name | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Orthographic / no translation impact | No alert. |
| 1 | Greek difference with no likely English impact | Log silently; do not interrupt the editor. |
| 2 | Minor English impact | Show in the packet; no default footnote. |
| 3 | Meaningful translation decision | Requires human check before finalizing. |
| 4 | Reader-visible, theological, or KJV-noticeable issue | Requires Decision Log entry and likely footnote. |
| 5 | Major passage-level issue | Requires special review before finalization. |

Examples of Level 4–5 issues include additions or omissions that readers may know from KJV/NKJV tradition, major bracketed passages, or variants that affect doctrine, Christology, syntax, or paragraph-level meaning.

The reading view should not be overloaded with textual-critical notes. The study view carries fuller variant data. Reader-facing notes are used only when the variant meaningfully affects the English text or when readers are likely to notice the difference from a familiar tradition.

## 7. Translation Philosophy

FLT translates **meaning over form**.

FLT sits firmly on the dynamic-equivalence side of the translation spectrum, closest in aim to the NLT: clear, natural, meaning-driven English that remains accountable to the Greek source through traceability, notes, and review.

Governing rules:

1. Translate as simply and plainly as possible when that still produces accurate, clear, natural English.
2. Translate dynamically — reordering, splitting sentences, supplying connectors, clarifying implied relationships, and rendering idiom for sense — when literal wording would be confusing, misleading, archaic, or foreign-sounding.
3. Preserve formal elements such as word order, repetition, imagery, and rhetorical structure when they remain clear and meaningful in English.
4. Do not drift so far toward paraphrase that the rendering can no longer be traced to the Greek word, phrase, or clause it carries.
5. Do not turn interpretation into translation when the Greek meaning should remain open.

### Minimum-Departure Principle

FLT lives in dynamic territory, but each verse should travel the **minimum distance from the Greek required for clarity**, not the maximum distance the register allows.

The discipline is:

1. Consider the plainer, more literal rendering first.
2. Depart toward a freer rendering only when comprehension for the target reader genuinely requires it.
3. Stop departing as soon as the comprehension problem is solved.
4. Surface significant departures through notes, glossary entries, or decision logs.

This is a deliberate brake against over-translation and unnecessary smoothing.

---

## 8. Relationship to NLT-Like Readability

FLT aims for readability and pastoral accessibility comparable to the NLT, but it is an **independent translation**.

NLT-like process principles may be adopted:

- clear contemporary English
- common vocabulary
- read-aloud testing
- multiple reviewers with different strengths
- refinement for precision without sacrificing clarity
- willingness to translate dynamically when literal wording obscures meaning

NLT wording is not a source and must not be reproduced.

---

## 9. Independence from Copyrighted Wording

Every FLT rendering must be independently justifiable from the Greek, the Constitution, the Style Guide, the Glossary, and the editor’s own judgment.

Copyrighted modern translations may be consulted for awareness only. They may not be used as drafting sources.

Each chapter must pass a Copyright-Independence Check:

1. Is the wording independently generated?
2. Does it avoid shadowing the NLT or any copyrighted version too closely?
3. Could the same English wording be defended directly from the Greek and FLT governing documents?
4. Did the human editor make the final wording decision?

AI-assisted drafts are raw material. The final Editor line must be the human editor’s own wording, never a verbatim adoption of a model’s output by default.

---

## 10. Free-Sharing and Licensing Goal

FLT is intended for free digital use — quotation, teaching, study, Scripture-engagement tools, apps, websites, and other digital pathways — with as few restrictions as legally possible.

The intent is fixed: FLT should be freely shareable.

> **Pending decision: license instrument.** The exact license is not yet chosen. Candidates include CC0 / public-domain dedication where legally possible, CC BY, CC BY-SA, or a custom Scripture-sharing license. The decision must be made deliberately before first public release.

---

## 11. Editorial Authority

The human editor is the sole final authority on every wording decision.

AI models — GPT, Claude, Gemini, and any others — serve only as assistants, reviewers, challengers, and summarizers. Their job is to help the editor see more, not to decide.

No model output becomes part of FLT by virtue of being generated. It becomes part of FLT only when the human editor enters it as a decision.

---

## 12. Use of AI Tools

Models are assigned roles by task, not by brand:

- Clarity Translator
- Greek Guardian
- Balanced Editor
- Red-Team Reviewer
- Committee Secretary

The same source text, project excerpt, glossary rules, and known issues are given to each model. Divergence in their output should reveal real translation tradeoffs rather than prompt noise.

### Honest Limitation

The AI model committee is a **simulation of peer review**, not a substitute for a real translation committee.

Human translation committees benefit from scholars with different training, traditions, denominational backgrounds, linguistic instincts, and theological concerns. AI models may imitate some of that process, but they share overlapping training data and may share hidden biases.

Therefore, FLT should eventually recruit actual human reviewers before any release is called authoritative.

---

## 13. Greek Fidelity and Traceability

FLT is a dynamic translation, but it remains accountable to the Greek.

Every rendered English word or phrase should remain traceable to the Greek word, phrase, clause, or semantic unit it carries through preserved Strong’s numbers and/or alignment metadata.

This makes FLT compatible with:

1. **Reverse interlinear view** — English-ordered text with Greek tags.
2. **Parallel study view** — FLT reading, formal gloss, and Strong’s spine.
3. **Formal gloss layer** — a Greek-ordered accountability aid, not the reader-facing translation.

A strict word-under-word interlinear is not appropriate for FLT because a dynamic translation reorders, expands, condenses, and supplies words for sense.

### Transparency Through Apparatus

Formal translations often pursue transparency to the original in the body text. FLT pursues readability in the body text and transparency through the apparatus.

The prose serves the reader. The apparatus serves accountability. Neither should erase the other.

---

## 14. Readability Commitments

Readability is a first-order obligation.

The Style Guide governs the detailed mechanics, but the constitutional commitments are:

1. Use clear contemporary English.
2. Prefer short, natural sentences unless the passage’s rhetoric requires longer movement.
3. Break long Greek periods into understandable English sequences.
4. Make implicit logical connectors explicit when needed for comprehension.
5. Render opaque idioms for sense.
6. Avoid quickly dated slang and narrow subdialects.
7. Read every chapter aloud before finalization.
8. Prefer the simpler faithful rendering when a fuller rendering would overload the reader.
9. Move fuller explanation to notes when necessary.

Working reading-level target: approximately Flesch-Kincaid grade 6, tolerating grade 7–8 where fidelity, theological vocabulary, or dense argument requires it.

---

## 15. Theological Terms and Glossary Authority

Key terms are governed by the Glossary, not by flat one-to-one mappings.

The Glossary uses tiers:

- **Tier 1 — Keep:** terms clear enough or important enough to retain.
- **Tier 2 — Context-weighed:** terms with several possible renderings chosen by context.
- **Tier 3 — Unpack:** terms likely to be unknown or misleading to the target reader.

Constitution-level invariants:

1. **εὐαγγέλιον** must always carry the sense of an announcement of something God has done.
2. **“Propitiation” is forbidden** in the body text.
3. The glossary must preserve judgment-call transparency through footnotes, notes, or decision-log entries.

The full handling of terms such as χάρις, πίστις, δικαιοσύνη, σάρξ, πνεῦμα, κύριος, Χριστός, ἅγιοι, μετάνοια, νόμος, κόσμος, and σωτηρία belongs in the Glossary.

---

## 16. Gender, Person, and Number

FLT may recast masculine generics where the Greek clearly intends a general human referent.

Permitted dynamic moves include:

- masculine singular generic → plural
- third person → second person where clarity and directness require it
- noun → verb or verb → noun where English clarity requires it
- generic “brothers” → “brothers and sisters” when the whole church is addressed

These moves must be triggered by meaning and comprehension, not by style preference alone.

Absolute exception: masculine nouns and pronouns referring to God are not neutralized.

Deity pronouns referring to God or Christ are capitalized in the FLT reading tradition unless a future style-guide decision deliberately changes that policy.

---

## 17. OT Quotations and Allusions

Direct Old Testament quotations in the New Testament should be visually marked in a consistent way.

Allusions and echoes are not automatically marked typographically, because identifying an allusion is a judgment call. Significant allusions may be surfaced through notes.

The Style Guide governs:

- whether OT quotations appear in small caps, indentation, or another form
- whether marking appears in the reading view, study view, or both
- how allusion notes are worded
- how quotation/citation data is stored

---

## 18. Divine-Name Policy

In the New Testament’s own voice, Greek κύριος is rendered **Lord** when referring to God or Christ, and **lord / master / sir** where the referent is human and context requires.

For Old Testament quotations where the underlying Hebrew has the Tetragrammaton (YHWH) but the Greek New Testament reads κύριος, FLT follows a hybrid rule:

1. Translate the Greek form as **Lord**.
2. Mark it semantically as a divine-name quotation instance.
3. Surface the Hebrew background through typography and/or a footnote.

The source file should encode the instance semantically rather than hard-coding a single typographic form:

```text
{{divine-name:Lord}}
```

The reading view may later render this as small caps, LORD, Lord with a footnote, or another consistent visual form.

Where FLT later translates Hebrew source text directly outside the New Testament project, “Yahweh” may be used. Within the New Testament, the normal policy is to translate the Greek text in front of us and surface the Hebrew background rather than silently replacing κύριος with Yahweh.

---

## 19. Footnote Principles

The Style Guide governs the exact taxonomy and wording of footnotes, but constitutionally FLT uses notes to preserve honesty.

Footnotes should surface:

- dynamic departures from literal form when helpful
- weighted-term decisions
- debated alternatives
- traditional renderings readers may expect
- significant textual variants
- OT quotation and divine-name background
- cultural or historical information necessary for comprehension

Footnotes should not overwhelm the reading experience. The reading view may carry a limited note set; the study view carries fuller transparency.

## 20. Review and Revision Process

Each chapter moves through the Keeper Workflow:

```text
Translation Packet
      ↓
Blind Drafts
      ↓
Self-Critique
      ↓
Cross-Critique
      ↓
Hybrid Suggestions
      ↓
Decision Brief
      ↓
Human Final Decision
      ↓
Style Guide / Glossary / Decision Log Update
```

Each chapter must also pass the chapter-level Quality Checks:

1. Semantic-proposition check.
2. Back-translation check.
3. Read-aloud check.
4. Key-term consistency check.
5. Copyright-independence check.
6. Variant-alert review where applicable.

### Fatigue Rule

One major editorial pass per sitting. Let the pass cool before the next pass.

This is a binding process rule because tired stacking of passes has already produced field mismatches, capitalization regressions, and footnote/text mismatches.

---

## 21. Standing Feedback Channel

FLT cannot initially replicate a standing translation committee, but it should adopt the principle of outside correction.

The project should maintain a defined channel for reviewer and reader feedback. Feedback should feed into the revision queue rather than being handled casually or lost.

This channel is the seed of the eventual human peer-review layer.

---

## 22. Definition of “Finalized”

A chapter is finalized only when all of the following hold:

1. It has completed the Keeper Workflow.
2. Significant decisions are recorded.
3. It has passed the Quality Checks.
4. Weighted-term renderings conform to the Glossary or carry documented justification.
5. Variant alerts at Level 3 or higher have been checked and resolved.
6. Constitutional invariants are satisfied.
7. Text, notes, footnotes, and editor fields are internally consistent.
8. The chapter has been read aloud in full.
9. The file Status line reads `finalized`.
10. It is the highest-numbered finalized version for that chapter.

Finalization is revisable. Later precedent may reopen a chapter, but reopening is a deliberate, logged act.

---

## 23. Revision Cadence

“Finalized” does not mean falsely permanent.

Meaning-based translations refine over time. FLT adopts the same posture: the whole translation carries a version number, and periodic refinement passes are expected.

Revision is not failure. It is part of faithful translation stewardship.
